Your Leaders Should Eventually Stop Needing You

If every important decision still comes back to you, you have built dependence—not leadership.

By Micheal Sheehy · 5 min read · July 2026

Leadership note

On succession, decision rights and the mixed emotions of a team that can lead without you.

← All insights

There is a moment in leadership that is both deeply satisfying and slightly unnerving: realizing your team could operate without you.

I have watched leaders grow into roles where they no longer needed me to resolve every difficult issue. They understood the standard, had built their own judgment and knew when to decide and when to escalate. My first reaction was pride. The quieter reaction was: well, what exactly am I here for now?

That small flash of anxiety is worth paying attention to. Senior leaders often say they want empowered teams while unconsciously preserving the decisions that keep them indispensable.

Ownership is not delegated administration

Giving somebody genuine ownership means accepting that they may reach a good decision differently from you. It means defining the outcome, principles and boundaries clearly, then leaving room for judgment.

Delegation fails when the leader gives away activity but keeps authority. The team prepares the recommendation, attends the meetings and manages the follow-up—but the real decision still waits for the executive. That creates the appearance of empowerment and the operating reality of a queue.

The test of leadership is not how many decisions require your presence. It is how many good decisions your system can make without it.

What leaders need from the CCO

They need clarity about risk appetite and non-negotiable standards. They need access to evidence, not folklore about what senior management might prefer. They need permission to surface uncertainty before it becomes a problem. And they need to know that a reasonable decision, made within authority and with the available facts, will be supported even if the outcome is imperfect.

That last point matters. Organizations cannot ask people to exercise judgment and then punish every outcome that looks different with hindsight. Accountability should examine the quality of the decision process, not only whether luck was kind.

The CCO’s job changes

As leaders become stronger, the CCO should move upward and outward: connecting risks across functions, building executive alignment, spending time with regulators and boards, developing future leaders and asking questions the operating cadence may be too busy to see.

This is not becoming less relevant. It is moving from being the answer to building an organization capable of finding better answers.

The best teams I have worked with did not need me in every room. They needed me to create the architecture in which their expertise could become authority—and to remain available when the decision genuinely exceeded it.

If your leaders eventually stop needing you for the work you hired them to own, that is not a threat to your role.

It is evidence that you have done it.
