Why Escalation Is Evidence of a Healthy Culture

A quiet compliance organization is not necessarily a healthy one.

By Micheal Sheehy · 5 min read · July 2026

Leadership note

On making uncertainty safe to surface before it becomes expensive.

← All insights

Leaders often say they want people to speak up. Employees watch what happens to the first person who does.

If that person is interrogated for not having every fact, treated as the owner of the problem or quietly excluded from future decisions, the organization has learned something—just not the lesson the leader intended.

In compliance, escalation is frequently discussed as a failure signal. A case crossed a threshold. A control broke. A regulator may need to be informed. But the act of escalation is not the failure. Done early and honestly, it is evidence that the organization can see itself.

Silence is not a metric of control

Low escalation volume can mean that processes are working. It can also mean thresholds are unclear, regional teams do not trust headquarters, bad news is being polished on the way upward or employees have concluded that raising a concern creates more personal risk than leaving it alone.

That is why I am cautious about celebrating quiet dashboards. The question is not simply how many issues were escalated. It is whether the right issues moved at the right speed, with enough evidence for the right person to act.

Healthy culture does not eliminate uncomfortable information. It shortens the distance that information must travel.

What leaders teach in the moment

The first response to an escalation is cultural infrastructure. “Thank you for raising it” sounds simple, but it matters—provided it is followed by disciplined action.

I want to know what is known, what remains uncertain, what immediate containment may be required and who is accountable for the next decision. I do not want an employee to spend three days perfecting a deck while the underlying risk keeps moving.

Nor should escalation become a way to transfer ownership upward. A healthy system distinguishes between visibility, consultation and decision authority. Leaders can know about a problem without taking it away from the person best placed to manage it.

Designing for candor

Clear triggers help. So do direct routes that do not depend on organizational hierarchy. Calibration matters because people need examples of what “early enough” looks like. After-action reviews should examine whether information travelled effectively, not only whether the final outcome was acceptable.

Most importantly, senior leaders must resist the temptation to make every issue a referendum on somebody’s competence. Sometimes a good leader is bringing bad news precisely because the system is working.

A culture of escalation is not a culture of alarm. It is a culture in which uncertainty can become shared evidence before it becomes shared damage.
